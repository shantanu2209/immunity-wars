# Medical review — the brief for the reviewer, and where verdicts go

**Opened 9 September 2026.** This is the hand-written half of the medical review.
[`MEDICAL_REVIEW.md`](MEDICAL_REVIEW.md) is the other half: **1,229 claims, generated from the
content pack**, and regenerated by `pnpm medical:review` whenever the pack changes.

**Verdicts go in this file, not in that one.** The compilation is generated, so anything written
inside it is deleted by the next run without warning ([`FINDINGS.md`](FINDINGS.md) #65). Section 5
below is where a reviewer's findings are recorded, and it is empty until there is a reviewer.

---

## 1. What this is for

The game teaches real immunology to children, and `CLAUDE.md`'s first hard rule is that
**scientific accuracy is non-negotiable — every mechanic must be defensible to a scientist.**
Until now the only check on that has been Kartik writing it and Shantanu holding the line. Neither
is a clinician, and **nobody with medical training has read the app's claims.**

That is the gap this closes. It is being closed *before* the app is put in front of teachers or
funders, which is the right order: a claim that has been in front of a class is more expensive to
correct than one that has not.

## 2. Who we are asking for, and what we are asking

**A doctor, or a medical student who has done microbiology and immunology.** A student is enough
for most of it. Sections F, G and H benefit from someone further along; a student can flag doubt
without resolving it, which is useful on its own.

**What is being asked, precisely:**

> **Is any claim wrong, or misleading in a way that would matter?**

**What is NOT being asked, and saying so saves the reviewer's time:**

- **Not whether a simplification is complete.** Every line is compressed for a 13-year-old.
  "There is much more to it than this" is expected and is not a finding.
- **Not whether the game models immunology faithfully.** It is a board game. The Monocyte moves
  one space per turn; nothing does that. What matters is whether what it SAYS is true, not whether
  the mechanics are a simulation.
- **Not style, wording or reading level.** Those are ours.

## 3. Read in this order — the risk is not evenly spread

The compilation is ordered for structure. **The review should be ordered by risk**, and these are
not the same order. If a reviewer has one hour rather than ten, it should be spent here:

### First, and it is not close: `Prevent` and `Treat` in section F

**212 claims — 106 `Prevent` and 106 `Treat` — and the only ones in the app that a child could
act on outside the game.** Everything
else is either true or wrong; these can be true or wrong *and* consequential. Examples of the shape
that matters, from the compilation itself:

- `DISEASE/Rabies/Prevent` — "After a bite: wash it, and get the vaccine IMMEDIATELY."
- `DISEASE/Rabies/Treat` — "Post-exposure vaccine works BEFORE symptoms start. After that, nothing does."

Both look right, and both are the kind of sentence where being slightly wrong is not slight. The
specific questions:

1. Is any prevention advice **wrong**?
2. Is any treatment advice wrong, **out of date**, or **specific enough to be mistaken for medical
   guidance** when it should not be?
3. Does anything read as **reassuring about a condition that needs a doctor**?
4. Is anything **regionally wrong for India**, which is where this will be played?

### Second: the `Causes` field, same section

**106 claims describing symptoms.** The risk is the reverse of the above — a child recognising
their own symptoms in a card. Wrong symptoms are a problem; so is a list that would frighten
without cause, and so is one that would reassure without cause.

### Third: sections A to E — the claims the RULES make

**These are the ones a reviewer would never see by playing the game or reading the text**, because
nobody wrote them as sentences. They are stated in words in the compilation so they can be judged:

- **`tropism`** — which organs a disease can infect, and by implication which it cannot. 106 claims.
- **`class`** — the antigen class each disease is assigned to. This one drives a real mechanic:
  an antibody made against a class works on every disease in that class, so a wrong assignment
  teaches a wrong idea about cross-reactivity. 106 claims.
- **`route`** — how each disease enters the body. **96** claims: the deck holds 97 cards, and the
  97th is **Pathogen X**, which is deliberately fictional (the novel pathogen nobody has seen) and
  so carries no real-world claim. It is still reviewable through what the app SAYS about it, in
  `WHY/pathogenX` and `PLAY/library.pathogenX`.
- **The cells** — the seven display names and what each is said to do. A wrong cell name is
  repeated more often than any sentence in the app, because it is what the player calls the piece
  for the whole game.

### Fourth: sections G to J — flavour and explanation

Crisis events, the fifteen "why it works this way" boxes, How to play, and in-play text. Lower risk
because they are general rather than actionable, but section H is what a judge at a science fair
would most likely question, so it is worth a careful pass if there is time.

### The shape of the 1,229, so the size is not a surprise

Counted from the generated document rather than estimated:

| Where | Claims | Made of |
|---|---|---|
| **F — the 106 diseases** | **974** | **560 prose** (106 each of `Discovered` `Causes` `Found` `Prevent` `Treat`, plus 30 card facts) and **414 from the rules** (106 tropism, 106 class, 96 route, 106 stats) |
| A to E | 113 | 49 cells, 19 pathogen types, 12 antigen classes, 21 organs, 12 routes |
| G to J | 142 | 16 events, 15 why boxes, 46 How to play, 65 in-play text |
| **Total** | **1,229** | |

**Four fifths of the review is section F**, and **212 of its 974 are the two fields that matter
most**. That is the whole argument for the reading order above.

## 4. Three things the reviewer should know before starting

- **Some simplifications are deliberate and already known.** If something looks compressed to the
  point of being wrong, say so anyway — but it may already be a recorded decision rather than an
  error.
- **"I am not sure" is a useful answer.** A flagged doubt costs us a look. A wrong claim left in
  costs us the accuracy rule. Please flag rather than resolve.
- **A correction can be applied from the claim id alone.** Quoting `DISEASE/Rabies/Treat` is
  enough; no file paths, no line numbers, no need to find where it appears in the app.

## 5. Findings — EMPTY, and stays empty until a reviewer has read it

**No clinician has reviewed any claim in this app as of 9 September 2026.** This section is the
record and it is deliberately blank rather than absent: an empty findings table is a visible
statement that the review has not happened, and a missing one is not.

| Claim id | Reviewer | Verdict | What is wrong | Action taken |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *(none yet)* | | | | |

**Nothing in this repository may claim the science has been reviewed until this table has entries
and a named reviewer.** That includes grant material, the About screen, and anything said to a
judge. `CLAUDE.md`'s standing rule applies with full force here: **never overclaim to judges or
funders**, and "reviewed by a doctor" is exactly the kind of claim that would be repeated back.

## 6. What is NOT in the compilation, stated so it is not assumed to be covered

- **The rulebook, quick reference and study packet** (`docs/*.docx`). They are Kartik's source
  documents and they overlap the app heavily, but they are not generated from the pack and are not
  compiled here. The fifteen "why" boxes ARE included, because those are pinned to the rulebook
  word for word by `why-boxes.test.ts` — so reviewing them reviews the rulebook's boxes too.
- **The board's anatomy as drawn** — which organ sits on which branch, and the route lengths. The
  organ-to-branch mapping is in the compilation as part of section D; the *drawn* anatomy of the
  A2 board is not, and would need someone to look at the artwork.
- ⚠️ **THE ILLUSTRATIONS, and this is the largest thing the compilation cannot reach.** There are
  29 illustrated assets (89 files across 1×/2×/3×) drawing the seven cells and the pathogen types.
  **A picture makes a claim as surely as a sentence does** — a macrophage drawn with the wrong
  morphology, or a "bacterium" drawn as a virion, teaches the wrong thing to a child who will
  remember the picture long after the text. None of it is text, so none of it is in
  `MEDICAL_REVIEW.md`, and no amount of regenerating that file will ever include it.
  **If the reviewer has time for one thing beyond the text, it should be looking at the art
  alongside the cell cards in section A.** Provenance for every asset is in
  [`ASSETS.md`](ASSETS.md).
- **The engine's log lines.** Roughly 183 strings that narrate play. They are mostly mechanical
  ("Already at the bloodstream hub") and the biological ones largely restate section J. ⚠️ **They
  are also the one place a correction is expensive**: engine player text is compared byte for byte
  against the legacy build by the equivalence corpus, so changing it is a deliberate, isolated
  engine change rather than a text edit (`CLAUDE.md`). If a reviewer finds a problem there it is
  still a finding — it just costs more to fix, and the cost is not a reason to leave it wrong.
- **Anything about how the immune system works that the app does NOT say.** An omission is only a
  finding here if what is said becomes misleading without it.

## 7. How to hand this to a reviewer

The compilation is a single markdown file, 189 KB, and it is readable as plain text. Practical
options, cheapest first:

1. **Send the file.** Works for anyone comfortable with markdown; no setup.
2. **A web page.** The same content as a browsable page with the sections separated, which is
   easier for a reviewer working in passes over several sittings. Not built; ask if wanted.
3. **Split by section.** F alone is most of the document, and F is most of the risk. A reviewer
   with limited time can be sent F and nothing else, honestly, provided they are told that is
   what they have.

**Whatever is sent, send section 3 of this document with it.** The compilation does not say what
to look at first, and a reviewer who starts at claim 1 and stops at claim 200 will have reviewed
the cells and the pathogen types and none of the treatment advice.
